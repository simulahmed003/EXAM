How to use this primer

This booklet teaches thermodynamics from zero, in the order the exam needs it. Read it once before the Complete Solutions. Each section has a green core idea box (learn this first), the key terms, the formulas with every symbol and unit, and a short worked example. Section 2 is a maths and units refresher: do not skip it, because most lost marks in this course come from unit slips and table reading.

The pass set (from the Question Map): Section A topics A1 (basic concepts), A2 (energy, heat and work), A3 (pure substance), A5 (first law), A6 (device numericals), A7 (second law and Carnot), A8 (entropy); Section B topics B2 and B3 (Rankine theory and numericals), B4 (IC engine cycles), B7 (gas mixtures) and B8 (psychrometry). Those topics are enough to write three full questions in each section.

The whole course on one page

Read the map from top to bottom: first you learn the words (system, property, state), then the substances (water/steam from tables, ideal gases from Pv = RT), then the laws (first law = energy is conserved, second law = direction and quality), and finally you apply them to cycles (steam plant, engines, gas turbine, refrigerator) and to mixtures (gas mixtures, moist air).

Maths, physics and units refresher

Almost every numerical is: write the right equation → get properties from a table or a formula → substitute with consistent units. Use SI: kPa, m3, kg, K, kJ. Then kPa × m3 = kJ automatically.

Logs and powers (needed for gas processes)

  • ln(a/b) = ln a − ln b; ln(an) = n ln a; eln x = x. Example: ln 2 = 0.693, ln 10 = 2.303.
  • Powers: x0.2857 appears in every isentropic air problem because (γ − 1)/γ = 0.4/1.4 = 0.2857. On a calculator: 8 [xy] 0.2857 = 1.811.
  • To undo a power: if r0.4 = 1.886 then r = 1.8861/0.4 = 1.8862.5.

Linear interpolation (needed for steam tables)

y = y1 + (x − x1)(y2 − y1)/(x2 − x1)

Example: h at 3 MPa, 355 °C, with h(350 °C) = 3115.3 and h(400 °C) = 3231.7: h = 3115.3 + (5/50)(116.4) = 3127.0 kJ/kg.

Reading the steam tables

  1. Decide the region: compare the given T with Tsat at the given p (or v with vf and vg).
  2. T < Tsat: compressed liquid → use h ≈ hf at T. T = Tsat with a quality: wet mixture → saturated table, h = hf + x hfg. T > Tsat: superheated table at p and T.
  3. Saturated tables come in two forms: by temperature (enter with T) and by pressure (enter with p). Subscripts: f = saturated liquid, g = saturated vapour, fg = g − f.
  4. For an isentropic process, carry s across: s2 = s1, then x2 = (s2 − sf)/sfg.

Calculus you need

Work is the area under a curve: W = ∫p dV. For p = C/V (isothermal ideal gas) ∫dV/V = ln(V2/V1). For p = C/Vn, ∫V−ndV = (V21−n − V11−n)/(1 − n). A partial derivative (∂p/∂T)v means "how p changes with T while v is held constant".

Systems, properties and states (A1)

Thermodynamics studies energy and its conversion. First choose the system (what you study) and its boundary. A system is closed if no mass crosses the boundary, open if mass flows through, isolated if neither mass nor energy crosses. A property (p, T, v, u, h, s) describes the state; a process changes the state; a cycle returns to the start.

  • Intensive properties do not depend on size (T, p, ρ); extensive ones do (m, V, U). Small letters (v, u, h, s) are per kg.
  • Equilibrium: nothing changes if the system is left alone; needs mechanical, thermal, chemical and phase equilibrium.
  • Quasi-equilibrium process: so slow that the system passes through equilibrium states; it can be drawn as a line on a diagram.
  • Steady flow: conditions inside a device do not change with time (a running turbine). Transient: they do (a tank being filled).

Energy, heat and work (A2)

A system stores energy as kinetic (motion), potential (height) and internal energy U (molecular motion and bonds). Energy crosses the boundary only as heat Q (driven by a temperature difference) or work W (any other cause: moving piston, shaft, electricity). Q and W are not properties: their amounts depend on the path.

E = KE (kinetic energy) + PE (potential energy) + U = mV2/2 + mgz + U

Example: an electric heater in a room. If the system is the room air, energy enters as heat (the hot element warms the air). If the system includes the heater, energy enters as electrical work through the wire. Same energy, different label: always define the system first.

Pure substances and the steam tables (A3)

A pure substance has the same chemical composition everywhere (water, even as ice + water + steam). When water is heated at constant pressure it goes: compressed liquid → saturated liquid → wet mixture (boiling at constant Tsat) → saturated vapour → superheated vapour. The T-v diagram shows these regions under and around a dome. Two independent properties fix the state (state postulate).

x = mvapour/mtotal v = vf + x(vg − vf) h = hf + x hfg

Ideal and real gases (A4)

At low pressure and high temperature, gases obey Pv = RT. Each gas has its own R = Ru/M. Near the critical point or at high pressure, real gases deviate; the compressibility factor Z = Pv/RT measures this, and equations such as van der Waals and Redlich-Kwong correct for molecular attraction (a) and size (b).

PV = mRT = nRuT R = Ru/M cp − cv = R γ = cp/cv

The first law (A5) and devices (A6)

Energy is conserved. Closed system: Q − W = ΔU (+ ΔKE + ΔPE). Steady-flow device: Q̇ − Ẇ = ṁ[(h2 − h1) + (V22 − V12)/2 + g(z2 − z1)]. Enthalpy h = u + pv includes the flow work needed to push fluid in and out.

The second law and heat engines (A7)

Heat flows naturally from hot to cold, and no engine can turn all the heat it receives into work: some must be rejected to a cold sink. The best possible (reversible, Carnot) engine between TH and TL has efficiency 1 − TL/TH. All real engines are less efficient because of irreversibilities (friction, heat transfer across finite ΔT, mixing).

Entropy (A8)

Entropy S is a property that measures disorder; it increases when heat is added and whenever there is irreversibility. In an isolated system entropy can never decrease (ΔS ≥ 0). An ideal adiabatic process has constant entropy (isentropic), and real machines are compared with it by the isentropic efficiency.

dS = (δQ/T)rev T ds = du + p dv = dh − v dp S2 − S1 = ∫δQ/T + Sgen

Exergy and property relations (A9, A10)

Exergy is the maximum useful work a system can give as it comes into equilibrium with the environment (the dead state, 25 °C and 1 atm). Energy is always conserved, but exergy is destroyed by every irreversibility: Xdestroyed = T0Sgen. Maxwell relations let us compute entropy changes from measurable p, v, T data.

Power cycles: Rankine, Otto, Diesel, Brayton (B1-B5)

A power cycle repeats four processes: compress → add heat → expand → reject heat. The Rankine cycle (steam plant) pumps liquid, boils it, expands steam in a turbine and condenses it. The Otto (petrol), Diesel and Dual cycles model piston engines; the Brayton cycle models gas turbines. Efficiency rises when heat is added at a higher average temperature or rejected at a lower one.

Refrigeration (B6)

A refrigerator uses work to move heat from a cold space to warm surroundings. In vapour compression, a refrigerant evaporates (absorbing heat) at low pressure, is compressed, condenses (rejecting heat) at high pressure and is throttled back. In vapour absorption, the compressor is replaced by an absorber, pump and generator driven by heat.

Gas mixtures and moist air (B7, B8)

A mixture of ideal gases behaves like one ideal gas with an average molar mass Mm = ΣyiMi. Each gas exerts its own partial pressure pi = yip (Dalton). Moist air is dry air + water vapour; its moisture is described by specific humidity ω, relative humidity φ and dew point, found from dry- and wet-bulb readings with Carrier’s equation.
